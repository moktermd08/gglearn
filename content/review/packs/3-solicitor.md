# Review pack: Solicitor or legal adviser

**For:** a solicitor who advises small businesses and charities.  **Items:** 8.  **Questions:** 32.

## What this is

A learning app for small businesses has a map of everything a business does, and each step (a "stop") has a learning track. These are **drafts** that an AI assistant wrote for steps where the app has little or no teaching material. **Nothing here is live.** Please read each one as someone who does this work, and say whether it is right.

They were written from general UK small-business knowledge, **not from cited documents**, so treat every fact as unchecked. Rules, thresholds and deadlines change, and some are different outside the UK.

## How to review

For each item, read the passage, then the questions. Tick the boxes, and write corrections in the spaces or straight onto the text. You do not need to fix everything; a note such as "wrong: the limit is X" is enough.

- ☐ **Accurate.** Is every statement in the passage true, and current?
- ☐ **UK only?** Would any of it be wrong or misleading outside the UK? Say which part.
- ☐ **Clear.** Would a small-business owner who knows nothing about this follow it?
- ☐ **Questions.** One clearly right answer, supported by the passage? Are the wrong answers plausible rather than silly?
- ☐ **Source.** Write down the real source you checked it against (a GOV.UK page, a regulator's guidance). The app needs one before this goes live.

**Verdict for each item:** Approve / Approve with changes / Reject. A "Reject" with a reason is as useful as an approval.

## 1. Co-founder and family agreements

*Step on the map:* Co-founder & Family Agreements  ·  *Learning track:* `executive-communication`  ·  *Id:* `family`

### The passage

> People who start a business together, as partners, friends or family, often avoid awkward conversations that later cause the biggest fights. Agree early, in writing: who owns what share, who does what, how decisions are made, how each is paid, what happens if one wants to leave, dies, becomes ill or stops contributing, and how disputes are settled. For a company, a shareholders' agreement can cover this; for a partnership, a partnership agreement. Revisit it as roles change. Family ties make clear agreements more important, not less. Take legal advice.

## 2. Patents and copyright

*Step on the map:* Patents & Copyright  ·  *Learning track:* `governance-compliance`  ·  *Id:* `ipdeep`

### The passage

> Intellectual property is what your ideas create. Copyright protects original writing, images, music, software and designs automatically when they are created; in the UK no registration is needed. The creator is normally the first owner, but work an employee makes in the course of their job usually belongs to the employer, and work made by a contractor belongs to the contractor unless the rights are assigned in writing. A patent protects a new invention and must be applied for; the idea must be kept secret until you file. A trademark protects a brand name or logo for particular goods or services. Patents and trademarks need specialist advice.

## 3. Registering the business

*Step on the map:* Register the Business  ·  *Learning track:* `governance-compliance`  ·  *Id:* `register`

### The passage

> How you register depends on your structure. A sole trader trades in their own name and registers with the tax authority; a partnership registers its partners; a limited company is formed with the company registry and gets its own legal identity, with directors, shareholders, a registered office and a duty to file accounts and a confirmation statement each year. Check that the name is available and is not misleading, and keep the company's records and registers up to date. Registering is the start of ongoing duties, not a one-off. Ask an accountant or formation agent if you are not sure.

## 4. Legal duties when closing a business

*Step on the map:* Legal Duties on Closing  ·  *Learning track:* `governance-compliance`  ·  *Id:* `legalclose`

### The passage

> Closing a business properly protects you. Tell the tax authority and any registry; file final returns and accounts; pay what you owe; and keep records for the required period. If you employ people, give proper notice and follow the redundancy rules, which can include consulting staff and paying redundancy pay and wages owed. Cancel contracts, leases and licences in line with their terms, and tell customers and suppliers. Close or transfer data lawfully: delete personal data you no longer need and keep what the law requires. If you cannot pay your debts, take advice from a licensed insolvency practitioner before doing anything.

## 5. Reviewing a contract

*Step on the map:* Contract Review  ·  *Learning track:* `governance-compliance`  ·  *Id:* `contractrev`

### The passage

> Reviewing a contract before you sign, or when it renews, can prevent expensive surprises. Read every clause and check the basics: the parties, what each side must do, the price and payment terms, how long it lasts and how it renews or ends. Look for clauses that limit liability, penalise you, allow price rises or lock you in. Check what happens if either side fails to perform. Mark anything unclear and ask for it to be changed in writing. Compare the written terms with what was agreed in conversation. For large or unusual contracts, have a solicitor review them.

## 6. Dissolving or transferring a business

*Step on the map:* Dissolve or Transfer  ·  *Learning track:* `governance-compliance`  ·  *Id:* `dissolve`

### The passage

> A business ends either by closing it or by transferring ownership. For a company, striking off or dissolving it usually requires that it has no debts or has settled them, has stopped trading, and has told the tax authority and other interested parties. You must file final accounts and returns and keep records. A sole trader stops trading and tells the tax authority. To transfer, such as a sale, agree the price, what is included, how liabilities and staff move, and put it in a written contract. Take advice from an accountant and a solicitor, because mistakes can leave you personally liable.

## 7. Trustees and board duties

*Step on the map:* Trustees & Board Duties  ·  *Learning track:* `governance-compliance`  ·  *Id:* `trustees`

### The passage

> People who sit on the board of a charity or company have legal duties. They must act within the powers in the governing document, in the interests of the organisation and its purpose, with reasonable care and skill, and avoid or declare conflicts of interest. Trustees of a charity share responsibility for it, must keep proper accounts and file returns with the regulator, and should not benefit personally except as the rules allow. A board should meet regularly, keep minutes, check performance and finances and make sure it has the skills it needs. Not knowing the duties is not a defence; read your governing document and the regulator's guidance.

## 8. Franchising and licensing

*Step on the map:* Franchising & Licensing  ·  *Learning track:* `product-management-core`  ·  *Id:* `franch`

### The passage

> Franchising lets others run a business under your brand and system in return for fees; licensing lets others use your brand, product or rights for a fee. Both can grow a business using other people's money and effort, but both need a proven, written system that others can copy, clear contracts, training and support. You give up some control, and franchisees' conduct affects your brand. Agree fees, territory, standards, what happens if they fail and how the agreement ends. Check the rules for your country, which may include disclosure duties. Take advice from a lawyer who knows franchising.
