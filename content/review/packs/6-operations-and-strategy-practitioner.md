## What this is

A learning app for small businesses has a map of everything a business does, and each step (a "stop") has a learning track. These are **drafts** that an AI assistant wrote for steps where the app has little or no teaching material. **Nothing here is live.** Please read each one as someone who does this work, and say whether it is right.

They were written from general UK small-business knowledge, **not from cited documents**, so treat every fact as unchecked. Rules, thresholds and deadlines change, and some are different outside the UK.

## How to review

For each item, read the passage, then the questions. Tick the boxes, and write corrections in the spaces or straight onto the text. You do not need to fix everything; a note such as "wrong: the limit is X" is enough.

- ☐ **Accurate.** Is every statement in the passage true, and current?
- ☐ **UK only?** Would any of it be wrong or misleading outside the UK? Say which part.
- ☐ **Clear.** Would a small-business owner who knows nothing about this follow it?
- ☐ **Questions.** One clearly right answer, supported by the passage? Are the wrong answers plausible rather than silly?
- ☐ **Source.** Write down the real source you checked it against (a GOV.UK page, a regulator's guidance). The app needs one before this goes live.

**Verdict for each item:** Approve / Approve with changes / Reject. A "Reject" with a reason is as useful as an approval.

## 4. Scaling operations

*Step on the map:* Scale Operations  ·  *Learning track:* `delivery-management`  ·  *Id:* `scaleops`

### The passage

> Scaling means handling more customers without a drop in quality or an equal rise in cost. First find the bottleneck, the step that limits output, and fix it first; improving anything else does not help. Write down and simplify processes so new people can follow them. Automate repeated, low-skill work and keep people for judgement and care. Train and supervise before you add volume. Watch quality and customer satisfaction as numbers grow, and add capacity ahead of demand but not too far ahead. Scale in steps and check after each, because what worked at ten customers often breaks at a hundred.

## 8. Policies and procedures

*Step on the map:* Policies & Procedures  ·  *Learning track:* `executive-communication`  ·  *Id:* `policies`

### The passage

> A policy says what the organisation expects and why; a procedure says how to do it, step by step. Write only what you need: data protection, health and safety, expenses, conduct, complaints. Keep each short and in plain words, name an owner, give a review date, and store them where everyone can find them. A policy nobody has read protects nobody, so tell people about it when they start and when it changes. Check that it works: ask someone to follow the procedure and see where they get stuck. If a rule is never followed, change the rule or change the way you enforce it.

### The passage

> A lessons-learned review turns experience into improvement. Hold it soon after a project, event or failure, with the people who were there, in a spirit of learning, not blame. Ask four things: what did we plan, what happened, why was there a difference, and what will we do differently. Look for causes in the process, not just in individuals. Write down a few clear actions with owners and dates, and share them with people who will face similar situations. Check later that the actions were done. Lessons that sit in a file and change nothing are not lessons.

## 12. Holding a quarterly review

*Step on the map:* Quarterly Review  ·  *Learning track:* `product-management-core`  ·  *Id:* `review`

### The passage

> A quarterly review is a short, regular meeting to check results against the plan and decide what to keep, change or stop. Gather the numbers beforehand: sales, margin, cash, and the progress of each goal. Spend the meeting on the differences between plan and result, ask why, and avoid reading out figures everyone has seen. End with decisions: a named owner and a date for each. Keep a one-page record, and carry unfinished items forward only if they still matter. Doing it every quarter, at a fixed time, catches problems while there is still room to act and makes the annual plan a living document.

## 15. Making improvements

*Step on the map:* Improvements  ·  *Learning track:* `service-design-pricing`  ·  *Id:* `improve`

### The passage

> Improving a product or service means choosing changes that matter to customers and checking that they worked. Start with evidence: complaints, returns, feedback and what staff see. List possible changes and pick the few with most benefit for the effort. Describe the result you want before you change anything, make the change, and measure the same thing afterwards. Make small changes often rather than large ones rarely, so a mistake is easy to undo. Record what you changed and why. If it did not help, learn from it and stop. Improvement is a habit, not a project.

## 16. Reviewing your range

*Step on the map:* Range Review  ·  *Learning track:* `service-design-pricing`  ·  *Id:* `range`

### The passage

> A range review looks at everything you sell and asks which products earn their place. For each, note sales, margin, how much time or stock it ties up, and how it fits what your customers want. Sort into those to keep and push, those to fix (price, quality, position), and those to stop. Be careful with products that sell well but make little profit once costs are counted, and with ones that bring customers in for other purchases. Decide with figures, not habit. Withdraw items properly: sell remaining stock, tell customers and honour what they have bought.
