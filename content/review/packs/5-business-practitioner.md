**For:** someone who has run or advised small businesses day to day.  **Items:** 28.  **Questions:** 112.

## What this is

A learning app for small businesses has a map of everything a business does, and each step (a "stop") has a learning track. These are **drafts** that an AI assistant wrote for steps where the app has little or no teaching material. **Nothing here is live.** Please read each one as someone who does this work, and say whether it is right.

They were written from general UK small-business knowledge, **not from cited documents**, so treat every fact as unchecked. Rules, thresholds and deadlines change, and some are different outside the UK.

## How to review

For each item, read the passage, then the questions. Tick the boxes, and write corrections in the spaces or straight onto the text. You do not need to fix everything; a note such as "wrong: the limit is X" is enough.

- ☐ **Accurate.** Is every statement in the passage true, and current?
- ☐ **UK only?** Would any of it be wrong or misleading outside the UK? Say which part.
- ☐ **Clear.** Would a small-business owner who knows nothing about this follow it?
- ☐ **Questions.** One clearly right answer, supported by the passage? Are the wrong answers plausible rather than silly?
- ☐ **Source.** Write down the real source you checked it against (a GOV.UK page, a regulator's guidance). The app needs one before this goes live.

**Verdict for each item:** Approve / Approve with changes / Reject. A "Reject" with a reason is as useful as an approval.

## 1. Making a launch plan

*Step on the map:* Launch Plan  ·  *Learning track:* `communications-email`  ·  *Id:* `launchplan`

### The passage

> A launch plan sets out what happens before, on and after the day you open to customers. Choose a realistic date, then work backwards: what must be ready (product, price, payment, legal, support), who does each task and by when. Decide who you want to tell and how: existing contacts, local people, an email list, social media, press. Prepare simple materials in advance, such as a short description, photos and a clear way to order. Plan for demand, because success needs stock and people to serve it. ### The passage

## 7. Scaling operations

*Step on the map:* Scale Operations  ·  *Learning track:* `delivery-management`  ·  *Id:* `scaleops`

### The passage

> Scaling means handling more customers without a drop in quality or an equal rise in cost. First find the bottleneck, the step that limits output, and fix it first; improving anything else does not help. Write down and simplify processes so new people can follow them. Automate repeated, low-skill work and keep people for judgement and care. Train and supervise before you add volume. Watch quality and customer satisfaction as numbers grow, and add capacity ahead of demand but not too far ahead. Scale in steps and check after each, because what worked at ten customers often breaks at a hundred.

## 11. Policies and procedures

### The passage

> A policy says what the organisation expects and why; a procedure says how to do it, step by step. Write only what you need: data protection, health and safety, expenses, conduct, complaints. Keep each short and in plain words, name an owner, give a review date, and store them where everyone can find them. A policy nobody has read protects nobody, so tell people about it when they start and when it changes. Check that it works: ask someone to follow the procedure and see where they get stuck. If a rule is never followed, change the rule or change the way you enforce it.

## 12. Running a strategic review

*Step on the map:* Strategic Review  ·  *Learning track:* `executive-communication`  ·  *Id:* `strategy`

### The passage

> A strategic review steps back from daily work to ask whether the business is heading the right way. Look at what has changed: customers, competitors, costs, technology and rules. Compare results with the goals, and ask honestly what is working, what is not and what you would not start today. Check the strengths you rely on and the risks you have been ignoring. Then choose a few priorities for the next phase and, just as important, decide what to drop. Write the conclusions and the reasons, share them, and set a date to check progress. A review that only confirms the current plan has probably not asked hard enough questions.

## 27. Checking your competitors

*Step on the map:* Competitor Check  ·  *Learning track:* `tools-business`  ·  *Id:* `rivals`

### The passage

> A competitor check shows what customers can choose instead of you. List direct competitors (the same product to the same customers), indirect ones (a different product solving the same problem) and substitutes, including doing nothing. The aim is not to copy; it is to choose where you will be clearly different, and to know what you are up against.

## 28. Partners and resellers

*Step on the map:* Partners & Resellers  ·  *Learning track:* `tools-business`  ·  *Id:* `partners`

### The passage

> A partner or reseller sells or recommends your product to their own customers, giving you reach without building it all yourself. Choose partners whose customers match yours and whose reputation you trust. Agree in writing what they may sell, where, at what price or commission, how they are paid and for how long, and how either side can end it. Give them what they need: information, training and samples. Check how they describe you, because their behaviour reflects on your brand. Track the sales each brings, and keep only partners that earn their place. Take legal advice on exclusive arrangements and territory.
