## How to review

- ☐ **Accurate.** Is every statement in the passage true, and current?

- ☐ **Clear.** Would a small-business owner who knows nothing about this follow it?

**Verdict for each item:** Approve / Approve with changes / Reject. A "Reject" with a reason is as useful as an approval.

## 1. Making an annual plan

*Step on the map:* Annual Plan  ·  *Learning track:* `executive-communication`  ·  *Id:* `annual`

### The passage

> An annual plan turns the strategy into what this year's team will actually do and spend. Start from last year's results and the few goals that matter most, not from a blank sheet. Set a small number of targets that can be measured, name an owner for each, and turn them into a budget: expected income, costs and the cash you will need month by month. Plan for what could go wrong with a sensible reserve, and decide in advance what you will stop doing to make room for new work. Share the plan so everyone can see how their work fits. ## 2. Controls and approvals

*Step on the map:* Controls & Approvals  ·  *Learning track:* `executive-communication`  ·  *Id:* `approvals`

### The passage

> Controls protect the business from mistakes and fraud without strangling it. Set clear limits for who can approve spending, signing contracts and paying people, with higher amounts needing more than one person. Separate duties so the person who orders is not the person who pays. Keep simple records of each approval. Review a sample of transactions regularly and reconcile bank accounts. Make rules proportionate: too many slow the work and invite workarounds, too few leave you exposed. Explain the reasons, and apply the same rules to the owner.

## 3. Bookkeeping for a small business

*Step on the map:* Bookkeeping  ·  *Learning track:* `finance-for-leaders`  ·  *Id:* `books`

### The passage

> Bookkeeping is recording every sale and every cost as it happens, so you can always say what the business earned and spent. Keep business money in its own bank account, keep every receipt and invoice, and record each transaction against a category such as sales, materials or rent. Once a month, reconcile: match your records to the bank statement and explain any difference. Under accruals accounting a sale is recorded when it is earned or invoiced, and a cost when it is incurred, even if cash moves later; under cash accounting it is recorded when money moves. Records must be kept for the period tax law requires, which in the UK is generally at least five to six years. Good books make tax, loans and selling the business far easier.

## 4. Ongoing tax and VAT

*Step on the map:* Ongoing Tax & VAT  ·  *Learning track:* `finance-for-leaders`  ·  *Id:* `taxrun`

### The passage

> Running a business means putting tax aside as you earn, not when the bill arrives. Set aside a fixed share of every payment in a separate account. Know which taxes apply: income tax or corporation tax on profit, VAT if you are registered (in the UK you must register once taxable sales pass the registration threshold, and may register earlier), and payroll taxes if you employ people. Each has deadlines and late penalties. VAT is collected for the tax authority: you charge it on sales, reclaim it on business purchases, and pay or reclaim the difference. Keep records that show how each figure was reached, and ask an accountant to confirm what applies to you.

## 5. Warning signs of trouble

*Step on the map:* Warning Signs of Trouble  ·  *Learning track:* `finance-for-leaders`  ·  *Id:* `insolv`

### The passage

> Most failing businesses show warning signs months before the end: cash that is lower every month, suppliers and tax paid late, one debt used to pay another, an overdraft always at its limit, customers who pay slower, a falling order book, and staff leaving. Watch a short list of figures every week: cash in the bank, money owed to you, and money you owe with its due dates. The earlier you act, the more options you have: talk to creditors, cut costs, raise money or restructure. Directors of a limited company have legal duties to creditors when insolvency is likely, so take advice from a licensed insolvency practitioner or accountant early, not at the last moment.

## 6. Paying creditors when a business closes

*Step on the map:* Pay Creditors & Distribute  ·  *Learning track:* `finance-for-leaders`  ·  *Id:* `creditors`

### The passage

> When a business closes, it must deal with what it owes before owners take anything. If it can pay all its debts (it is solvent), it pays creditors in full, and any money left can be shared among the owners. If it cannot pay everyone (it is insolvent), the law sets an order: the costs of the process first, then secured creditors, then certain preferential creditors (such as some employee claims), then unsecured creditors who share what is left in proportion, and owners last. Paying one creditor ahead of others in an insolvency can be challenged. An insolvent closure needs a licensed insolvency practitioner, so this is a point to take advice, not to guess.

## 7. Registering for tax

*Step on the map:* Tax Registration  ·  *Learning track:* `governance-compliance`  ·  *Id:* `tax`

### The passage

> Registering for tax tells the authorities you exist and which taxes you owe. A sole trader in the UK registers for self-assessment; a limited company is registered for corporation tax; an employer registers for PAYE; and VAT registration is required once taxable sales pass the threshold, and is optional below it. Register on time, because late registration can mean penalties. Keep the tax reference numbers safe and diarise the filing dates. Tax rules differ by country; ask an accountant which registrations apply to you and when.

## 8. Payroll and pensions

*Step on the map:* Payroll & Pensions  ·  *Learning track:* `people-operations`  ·  *Id:* `payroll`

### The passage

> If you employ someone you must run payroll. Each time you pay, work out gross pay, deduct income tax and employee National Insurance, add employer National Insurance, and report to the tax authority (in the UK, through PAYE, in real time). Pay at least the national minimum or living wage for the worker's age. Give each worker a payslip. Eligible workers must be enrolled automatically into a workplace pension, with the employer paying in too, and you must keep records. Mistakes cost the employee first, so use payroll software or a payroll provider and check with an accountant what applies to you.
