# Review pack: Sales and marketing practitioner

**For:** someone who has run sales or marketing for a small business.  **Items:** 12.  **Questions:** 48.

## What this is

A learning app for small businesses has a map of everything a business does, and each step (a "stop") has a learning track. These are **drafts** that an AI assistant wrote for steps where the app has little or no teaching material. **Nothing here is live.** Please read each one as someone who does this work, and say whether it is right.

They were written from general UK small-business knowledge, **not from cited documents**, so treat every fact as unchecked. Rules, thresholds and deadlines change, and some are different outside the UK.

## How to review

For each item, read the passage, then the questions. Tick the boxes, and write corrections in the spaces or straight onto the text. You do not need to fix everything; a note such as "wrong: the limit is X" is enough.

- ☐ **Accurate.** Is every statement in the passage true, and current?
- ☐ **UK only?** Would any of it be wrong or misleading outside the UK? Say which part.
- ☐ **Clear.** Would a small-business owner who knows nothing about this follow it?
- ☐ **Questions.** One clearly right answer, supported by the passage? Are the wrong answers plausible rather than silly?
- ☐ **Source.** Write down the real source you checked it against (a GOV.UK page, a regulator's guidance). The app needs one before this goes live.

**Verdict for each item:** Approve / Approve with changes / Reject. A "Reject" with a reason is as useful as an approval.

## 1. Making a launch plan

*Step on the map:* Launch Plan  ·  *Learning track:* `communications-email`  ·  *Id:* `launchplan`

### The passage

> A launch plan sets out what happens before, on and after the day you open to customers. Choose a realistic date, then work backwards: what must be ready (product, price, payment, legal, support), who does each task and by when. Decide who you want to tell and how: existing contacts, local people, an email list, social media, press. Prepare simple materials in advance, such as a short description, photos and a clear way to order. Plan for demand, because success needs stock and people to serve it. Afterwards, look at what worked, and thank the first customers. A launch is the start of a relationship, not a single event.

## 2. Launch day

*Step on the map:* Launch Day  ·  *Learning track:* `communications-email`  ·  *Id:* `open`

### The passage

> Launch day is the first real test of your plan, so make it simple and controlled. Check that everything works in advance: payment, ordering, signs, the website and opening hours. Make sure each person knows their job and who to call if something fails. Tell your customers clearly where, when and how to buy. Keep a record of what happens: how many people came, what they bought, what went wrong. Be ready to fix small problems immediately and to apologise when needed. Ask first customers for honest feedback and thank them. The day is not the finish; the days after show whether the business works.

## 3. Capturing enquiries

*Step on the map:* Capture Enquiries  ·  *Learning track:* `communications-email`  ·  *Id:* `leads`

### The passage

> An enquiry is a person telling you they might buy, so never let one go missing. Make it easy to ask: a clear phone number, a short form, a booking link, a message option. Send every enquiry to one place, such as a shared inbox or simple list, and record who it was, what they want and where they came from. Reply the same day if you can, because many people buy from the first business that answers. Follow up politely if they go quiet. Where you keep personal details, say what you will use them for and only send marketing to people who agreed to receive it.

## 5. Referrals and repeat sales

*Step on the map:* Referrals & Repeat Sales  ·  *Learning track:* `gglink-sales-support`  ·  *Id:* `referrals`

### The passage

> The cheapest customer to win is one you already have, or one a happy customer sends. Make repeat business easy: stay in touch, remind customers when they are due to buy again, and reward loyalty without discounting everything. Ask for referrals at the moment of delight, right after a good result, and make it easy to pass on, with a link or a card. Thank the person who referred. ## 6. Using customer feedback

*Step on the map:* Customer Feedback  ·  *Learning track:* `gglink-sales-support`  ·  *Id:* `cfeed`

### The passage

> Feedback is only useful if it changes something. Gather comments from every source in one place: surveys, reviews, emails, calls and what staff hear. Look for themes, because one comment is a story and ten the same is a pattern. Separate quick fixes from bigger changes and decide which to do first by how many customers it affects and how much it matters to them. Give each chosen change an owner and a date, and tell customers what you changed because of what they said. Keep the record, so you can see over time whether the same complaint returns.

*Step on the map:* Collect Feedback  ·  *Learning track:* `gglink-sales-support`  ·  *Id:* `feedback`

### The passage

> To learn what customers think, ask them and make it easy. Ask soon after the experience, while it is fresh, with a few short questions: one score, and one open question such as "What one thing would improve this?". Use more than one route: a quick message after a sale, a card, a call to a few customers, and review sites. Ask unhappy customers as well as happy ones, because they show what to fix. ## 9. Tenders and bids

*Step on the map:* Tenders & Bids  ·  *Learning track:* `gglink-sales-support`  ·  *Id:* `tender`

### The passage

> A tender is a formal invitation to offer to supply goods or services, often to public bodies and larger firms. First decide whether to bid: do you meet every must-have, can you deliver on time and can you profit at the likely price? Read the documents completely and note the deadline, because late bids are usually rejected. Answer every question directly, in the order and format asked, with evidence such as examples and figures. Show how you would deliver, and what could go wrong. Price to cover your costs and a fair margin. Check, ideally by someone who did not write it, and submit early.

Describe them specifically: who they are, what problem they have, what they do today to solve it, how they decide to buy, what they can afford and where you can reach them. ## 11. Checking your competitors

*Step on the map:* Competitor Check  ·  *Learning track:* `tools-business`  ·  *Id:* `rivals`

### The passage

> A competitor check shows what customers can choose instead of you. List direct competitors (the same product to the same customers), indirect ones (a different product solving the same problem) and substitutes, including doing nothing. For each, note what they offer, their price, who they serve, how they sell, and what customers praise or complain about in reviews. Mystery shop them if you can. Then ask where there is a gap: something customers want that nobody does well, or a group nobody serves. The aim is not to copy; it is to choose where you will be clearly different, and to know what you are up against.

## 12. Partners and resellers

> A partner or reseller sells or recommends your product to their own customers, giving you reach without building it all yourself. Choose partners whose customers match yours and whose reputation you trust. Agree in writing what they may sell, where, at what price or commission, how they are paid and for how long, and how either side can end it. Give them what they need: information, training and samples. Check how they describe you, because their behaviour reflects on your brand. Track the sales each brings, and keep only partners that earn their place. Take legal advice on exclusive arrangements and territory.
