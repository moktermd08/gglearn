## 2. Employment law basics

*Step on the map:* Employment Law Basics  ·  *Learning track:* `governance-compliance`  ·  *Id:* `employlaw`

### The passage

> Employment law sets minimum rights that you cannot contract out of. Know whether someone is an employee, a worker or self-employed, because their rights differ. Employees and workers are entitled to at least the minimum wage, paid holiday, rest breaks and limits on weekly working time (in the UK, an average of 48 hours unless they opt out). Everyone is protected from discrimination on protected characteristics such as age, sex, race and disability, and employers must make reasonable adjustments for disabled staff. You must check the right to work before someone starts, and give a written statement of terms. Rules and qualifying periods change, so check current guidance.

## 3. Discipline and dismissal

*Step on the map:* Discipline & Dismissal  ·  *Learning track:* `people-operations`  ·  *Id:* `hrproc`

### The passage

> Treat a performance or conduct problem as a process, not an outburst. Start by raising it clearly and informally. If it continues, investigate the facts, tell the employee in writing what the concern is, hold a meeting where they can explain and bring a companion, decide and give the outcome in writing, and offer a right to appeal. Warnings usually build in steps and are proportionate to the problem; serious misconduct can justify a faster route but still needs a fair investigation. Dismissal must be for a fair reason, such as conduct, capability or redundancy, and be handled fairly. Keep notes of every step. Take employment advice before any formal step, because rules and qualifying periods change.

## 4. Pay and progression

*Step on the map:* Pay & Progression  ·  *Learning track:* `people-operations`  ·  *Id:* `payreview`

### The passage

> A fair pay system is one people can understand. Set pay bands for each role, based on what the role does and what the market pays, and place people within a band by clear criteria such as skill and experience, not by who negotiates hardest. Employers must give equal pay for equal work or work of equal value regardless of protected characteristics such as sex, and unexplained gaps are a risk. Review pay on a regular cycle, explain the decision, and link progression to published criteria for the next level. Be open about how a rise or promotion is earned; secret, inconsistent pay breeds unfairness and turnover.

## 5. Recruiting and selecting fairly

*Step on the map:* Recruit & Select  ·  *Learning track:* `people-operations`  ·  *Id:* `recruit`

### The passage

> Hire on evidence, not impression. Write down what the job needs before you advertise: the tasks, the skills that matter and the pay. Use the same questions and the same scoring for every candidate, based on the role, and keep notes; this is fairer and more reliable than a relaxed chat. A short, realistic task shows more than a CV. Check references and the right to work before the start date. Do not ask about protected characteristics such as age, health or plans for children, and avoid choosing people because they remind you of yourself. Tell unsuccessful candidates promptly; it protects your reputation.

## 7. Stress and burnout at work

*Step on the map:* Stress & Burnout  ·  *Learning track:* `people-operations`  ·  *Id:* `stress`

### The passage

> Stress is a normal reaction to pressure, but long-lasting pressure with no recovery becomes burnout: exhaustion, cynicism and falling performance. Employers have a duty to look after employees' health, including from work-related stress. Signs include irritability, more mistakes, absence, withdrawal and very long hours. Prevent it by balancing workload with capacity, giving people control over how they work, setting clear expectations, supporting them as a manager, and allowing real rest. When someone shows signs, talk privately, listen, and change the work; do not just tell them to cope. For an individual who is unwell, point them to occupational health or their doctor.

## 8. Delegating and one-to-ones

*Step on the map:* Delegating & One-to-ones  ·  *Learning track:* `people-operations`  ·  *Id:* `delegate`

### The passage

> Delegating means handing over a task and the authority to do it, while you stay accountable for the result. Describe the outcome you want, not every step; give a deadline, the information and the authority needed, agree when you will check in, and then let the person do it their way. Taking a task back at the first stumble teaches people to wait for you. A one-to-one is a regular private meeting, often weekly or fortnightly, where the employee sets much of the agenda: what is in the way, what they need, how they are, and what feedback you can both give. Keep it even when busy; cancelling it tells people they are not a priority.

## 9. Culture and values

*Step on the map:* Culture & Values  ·  *Learning track:* `people-operations`  ·  *Id:* `culture`

### The passage

> Culture is how people actually behave when nobody is watching. Values only matter when they are lived. Choose a few values you really mean, describe them as behaviours people can see, and use them to decide who to hire, reward, promote and part with. Leaders set the culture most by what they do, so their conduct must match what they say. Notice what you tolerate: what is allowed becomes the norm. Ask staff how it feels to work there and listen. Culture changes slowly through consistent small actions, not posters or one-off events.

As the team grows, the founder cannot manage everyone: define roles, give managers authority and make decisions at the right level. Keep communication regular so people know the plan.
